    Displays, Light and Vision

    Visual Acuity

    Section titled “Visual Acuity”

    Let the eye’s minimum angle of resolution (MAR) be 𝜃 arcmin. If the corresponding visual acuity (UCVA / BCVA) is 𝑉 in decimal notation, then[2, Sec. 3.3.1]

    𝜃=1𝑉

    Thus, when 𝑉=1.0, the minimum angle of resolution is 1 arcminute; when 𝑉=2.0, it is 0.5 arcminutes.

    The minimum angle of resolution is measured using optotypes. For an E optotype, the width of each stroke represents the angular resolution[3, Sec. 2.2]; for a C optotype (Landolt ring), the width of the gap or the ring represents the angular resolution.[2, Fig. 1]

    Visual acuity can be expressed using notations other than decimal notation. For example, the mainland of China also uses the 5-mark record[3, Sec. 2.14]. It is calculated as

    𝐿=5−lg⁡𝜃

    For example, 4.0 represents an MAR of 10 arcminutes, 5.0 represents a MAR of 1 arcminute, and 5.3 represents a MAR of 0.5 arcminutes. Snellen fractions are also used in English-speaking regions[2, Sec. 3.3.2]. They are calculated as

    𝑉Sn=𝐷t𝐷n

    𝑉Sn, 𝐷t, and 𝐷n represent the (unreduced) Snellen fraction, the test distance, and the normal distance, respectively. The value of 𝑉Sn equals the value in decimal notation. For instance, 20/40 or 6/12 represents 2 arcminutes: The optotype that the subject can identify from 20 feet or 6 meters can be identified by a person with normal vision from 40 feet or 12 meters. Similarly, 20/8 or 6/2.4 represents 0.4 arcminutes: The optotype that the subject can identify from 20 feet or 6 meters requires a person with normal vision to approach to within 8 feet or 2.4 meters to identify it.

    LogMAR is one of the most commonly used notations in research literature. It is calculated as[2, Sec. 3.3.3]

    LogMAR=−lg⁡𝑉

    Decimal visual acuities of 0.2, 1.0, and 1.6 correspond to LogMAR values of +0.70, 0, and -0.20, respectively.

    Pixel Density

    Section titled “Pixel Density”

    Let 𝑙 be a unit length. The pixel density is

    𝜌=𝑙𝑝

    Substituting 𝑙=1in gives pixels per inch (PPI), while substituting 𝑙=1cm gives pixels per centimeter (PPCM).

    Estimating the Required Pixel Density from Visual Acuity

    Section titled “Estimating the Required Pixel Density from Visual Acuity”

    Let 𝑑 be the viewing distance and 𝑝 the pixel pitch. Then

    tan⁡(12𝜃)=12𝑝𝑑

    Therefore,

    𝑝=2𝑑tan⁡(12𝜃)

    We also have

    1arcmin=1180𝜋×160

    Eliminating 𝜃 gives

    𝜌=𝑙2𝑑tan⁡(𝜋21600𝑉)

    Thus, the required pixel density 𝜌 depends on both the visual acuity 𝑉 and the viewing distance 𝑑.

    Note that this is only an estimate. Pixel densities above this value can still be useful:

    • In general, the human eye has different sensitivities to points, lines, periodic textures, aliasing, etc.
    • Subpixel layouts[1, Fig. 1] such as RGB stripe and PenTile can have perceptible effects.
    • Contrast, viewing angle, software-level font anti-aliasing, and other factors can also have significant effects.
    • Binocular visual acuity is often better than monocular visual acuity.

    Superposition Principle

    Section titled “Superposition Principle”

    Consider a system 𝑦=𝐹(𝑥) with domain 𝐷, where 𝑥 and 𝑦 represent its input (stimulus) and output (response), respectively, and may be either scalars or vectors. ∀𝑥1,𝑥2∈𝐷 if the following always hold:

    • Additivity: 𝐹(𝑥1+𝑥2)=𝐹(𝑥1)+𝐹(𝑥2)
    • Homogeneity: 𝐹(𝑎𝑥)=𝑎𝐹(𝑥), where 𝑎 is any scalar

    then the system obeys the superposition principle. Additivity and homogeneity are collectively called linearity.

    The superposition principle becomes invalid under certain special circumstances[18, pp. 4–6][19, p. 1]. However, it is generally valid for conventional display technologies.

    Preliminaries of Radiometry

    Section titled “Preliminaries of Radiometry”

    Let 𝑄 be the cumulative energy emitted by a source of electromagnetic waves, and let 𝑡 denote a time point. If the source emits a total energy of Δ𝑄 during the time interval Δ𝑡, then its (instantaneous) radiant flux (radiant power) at a particular moment is[4, Sec. 4.1.1]

    Φe=Φe(𝑡)=limΔ𝑡→0⁡(Δ𝑄Δ𝑡)=d𝑄d𝑡

    The subscript e denotes a quantity in radiometry[8, Sec. 2].

    At a particular moment, let 𝐴 denote the area of a surface, 𝑃 a point on that surface, d𝐴 an area element containing 𝑃, and dΦe(𝑃) the (instantaneous) radiant power that the area element receives or emits from all directions. The received and emitted quantities are called irradiance 𝐸e(𝑃) and radiant exitance 𝑀e(𝑃), respectively; each is the area density of radiant power. Thus[4, Eq. 4.1][11]

    𝐸e(𝑃)=dΦe(𝑃)d𝐴𝑀e(𝑃)=dΦe(𝑃)d𝐴

    When the source is a point source, its radiant intensity in a direction 𝜔 (represented by a unit vector), i.e., the radiant power per unit solid angle, is[4, Eq. 4.2]

    𝐼e(𝜔)=dΦe(𝜔)d𝜔

    where d𝜔 is the differential solid angle around that direction[5, pp. 13, 16–17].

    Experiments show that, outside strong gravitational fields, meanwhile in a vacuum or a uniform isotropic medium without absorption, diffraction, scattering, or interfaces (hereafter called “ideal conditions”), electromagnetic waves can be approximated as traveling in straight lines. Their directions of propagation can be represented by rays. When the electromagnetic waves are visible light, these rays are called light rays.

    An electromagnetic wave does not necessarily pass through a surface perpendicularly. Let 𝑛⃗ be the normal vector at a point 𝑃 in the area element d𝐴, and let the wave arrive from direction 𝜔. Write 𝜃=∠(𝑛⃗,𝜔)=⟨𝑛⃗,𝜔⟩ s.t. 𝜃∈[0,12𝜋). The irradiance from direction 𝜔 is then

    d𝐸e⊥(𝑃,𝜔)=d𝐸e(𝑃,𝜔)cos⁡𝜃

    In other words, the area element d𝐴 would receive irradiance d𝐸e⊥(𝑃,𝜔) from direction 𝜔 only if it were perpendicular to 𝜔, i.e., 𝜃=0. If it is not perpendicular to the incident radiation—that is, if 𝜃>0—it receives only the lower irradiance d𝐸e(𝑃,𝜔)=d𝐸e⊥(𝑃,𝜔)cos⁡𝜃 from that direction. For example, sunlight can be treated as parallel light in everyday situations. A solar panel receives its maximum irradiance, and therefore can generate the most electricity, only when sunlight strikes it perpendicularly.

    Choose a point 𝑃 on a surface and an observation direction 𝜔. The radiance at 𝑃 in direction 𝜔 is[4, Eq. 4.3][5, p. 33]

    𝐿e(𝑃,𝜔)=d𝐸e⊥(𝑃,𝜔)d𝜔=d2Φe(𝑃,𝜔)d𝐴cos⁡𝜃 d𝜔

    d2Φe(𝑃,𝜔) represents the radiant power transmitted by the area element d𝐴 containing 𝑃 through a small solid angle d𝜔 around direction 𝜔.

    Under ideal conditions, let points 𝑃1 and 𝑃2 lie on area elements d𝐴1 and d𝐴2, respectively, separated by a distance 𝑟. Consider a steady beam of electromagnetic radiation traveling from 𝑃1 toward 𝑃2 in direction 𝜔. By conservation of energy, the power that this beam transmits through d𝐴1 and d𝐴2 is the same, denote it by d2Φe. Let the acute angles 𝜃1 and 𝜃2 be the angles between the propagation direction 𝜔 and the normal vectors of d𝐴1 at 𝑃1 and d𝐴2 at 𝑃2, respectively. Then

    𝐿e(𝑃1,𝜔)=d2Φed𝐴1cos⁡𝜃1d𝜔1,𝐿e(𝑃2,𝜔)=d2Φed𝐴2cos⁡𝜃2d𝜔2

    Here, d𝜔1 can be determined from d𝐴2 and 𝜃2, while d𝜔2 can be determined from d𝐴1 and 𝜃1:

    d𝜔1=d𝐴2cos⁡𝜃2𝑟2,ddω2=d𝐴1cos⁡𝜃1𝑟2

    Combining these equations gives

    d2Φe=𝐿e(𝑃1,𝜔)d𝐴1cos⁡𝜃1d𝜔1=𝐿e(𝑃1,𝜔)d𝐴1cos⁡𝜃1d𝐴2cos⁡𝜃2𝑟2d2Φe=𝐿e(𝑃2,𝜔)d𝐴2cos⁡𝜃2d𝜔2=𝐿e(𝑃2,𝜔)d𝐴2cos⁡𝜃2d𝐴1cos⁡𝜃1𝑟2

    Therefore,

    𝐿e(𝑃1,𝜔)=𝐿e(𝑃2,𝜔)

    This shows that, under ideal conditions, the radiance observed from the same steady source of electromagnetic waves remains constant along the direction in which the radiation propagates.[5, p. 32]

    Luminous Efficiency Functions

    Section titled “Luminous Efficiency Functions”

    Numerous experiments have shown that the human eye has different sensitivities to light at different wavelengths. Its sensitivity to a given wavelength also differs between well-lit and low-light environments[8, Fig. 1].

    Under photopic conditions, denote the (relative) spectral luminous efficiency function[7, p. 17] by 𝑉(𝜆)∈[0,1], where 𝜆 is the wavelength of the light. Outside the visible-light wavelength range, define 𝑉(𝜆)=0.

    The scotopic spectral luminous efficiency function 𝑉′(𝜆) is defined similarly.

    Luminous Flux, Luminous Exitance, and Illuminance

    Section titled “Luminous Flux, Luminous Exitance, and Illuminance”

    A light source can emit light at multiple wavelengths simultaneously. By the superposition principle, let the spectral distribution of the source’s radiant flux be Φe,𝜆(𝜆)=dΦed𝜆. Its luminous flux under photopic and scotopic conditions is, respectively,

    Φv=∫0+∞Φe,𝜆(𝜆)𝐾m𝑉(𝜆)d𝜆=𝐾m∫0+∞Φe,𝜆(𝜆)𝑉(𝜆)d𝜆Φv′=∫0+∞Φe,𝜆(𝜆)𝐾m′𝑉′(𝜆)d𝜆=𝐾m′∫0+∞Φe,𝜆(𝜆)𝑉′(𝜆)d𝜆

    The subscript v denotes a quantity in photometry[8, Sec. 2].

    The maximum luminous efficacies under photopic and scotopic conditions are

    𝐾m=𝐾cd𝑉(𝜆m)𝑉(𝜆a),𝐾m′=𝐾cd𝑉′(𝜆m′)𝑉′(𝜆a)

    In 2019, 𝐾cd was defined to be exactly 683lm/W[9, Sec. 2]. This means that converting 1 W of power entirely into visible light at 𝑓cd=540THz produces a luminous flux of 683 lm. Monochromatic radiation at frequency 𝑓cd is also specified to have wavelength 𝜆a=555.017nm in standard air[9, p. 3].

    Experiments show that under photopic and scotopic conditions, 𝑉(𝜆) and 𝑉′(𝜆) reach their respective peaks at wavelengths 𝜆m=555nm and 𝜆𝑚′=507nm. It follows that 𝐾m≈683.002lm/W[8, Eq. 4] and 𝐾m′≈1700.13lm/W[8, Eq. 6].

    Luminous flux emitted or reflected per unit area is called luminous exitance[12]

    𝑀v=dΦvd𝐴

    Luminous flux received per unit area is called illuminance[10]

    𝐸v=dΦvd𝐴

    A common unit of illuminance is the lux, lx, where 1lx=1lm/m2. The corresponding unit used for luminous exitance is simply lm/m2.

    Luminance and Brightness

    Section titled “Luminance and Brightness”

    By the superposition principle, let the spectral distribution of a source’s radiance be 𝐿e,𝜆(𝜆)=d𝐿ed𝜆. Its luminance is

    𝐿v=∫0+∞𝐿e,𝜆(𝜆)𝐾m𝑉(𝜆)d𝜆=𝐾m∫0+∞𝐿e,𝜆(𝜆)𝑉(𝜆)d𝜆

    A common unit of luminance is nit, which is equal to cd/m2, where 1cd=1lm/sr.

    Under ideal conditions, the spectral radiance 𝐿e,𝜆(𝜆) remains constant along a ray emitted by the same steady light source, so its luminance also remains constant. This agrees with everyday experience: Outdoors on a clear day with unchanged lighting conditions, approaching a white wall makes the wall cover more of the visual field, while moving away makes it cover less; nevertheless, the wall does not appear to become noticeably brighter or darker.

    Besides luminance, there is also a quantity called brightness. The difference is that luminance is a physically measurable photometric quantity, whereas brightness is affected by the background, contrast, visual adaptation, and other viewing conditions.

    Measurement

    Section titled “Measurement”

    An instrument that measures illuminance is called an illuminance meter; one that measures luminance is called a luminance meter. The main instruments used to measure luminous flux are integrating spheres and goniophotometers.

    In practice, measuring illuminance is far more convenient than measuring luminance, while measuring luminance is far more convenient than measuring luminous flux, which is usually measured in a specialized laboratory. Because measuring illuminance is comparatively inexpensive, nearly all mainstream smartphones include an illuminance sensor.

    Application: Screen Luminance and Projector Luminous Flux

    Section titled “Application: Screen Luminance and Projector Luminous Flux”

    Suppose a cinema auditorium uses a screen of area 𝐴. Assume that every point on the screen always has a reflectance[15] of 𝜌=1, an absorptance[17] of 𝛼=0, and a transmittance[16] of 𝜏=0, and that it is an ideal diffuse reflector (a Lambertian surface)[14]. The projector(s) and screen are engineered to make the luminance as uniform as possible across the screen.

    The screen reflects light into the forward hemisphere Ω. By Lambert’s cosine law[13], its luminance 𝐿 is independent of the viewing angle. Let 𝜃 be the angle between the viewing direction and the surface normal. The relationship between the screen’s total luminous exitance and its luminance is

    𝑀=∫Ω𝐿cos⁡𝜃 dΩs.t.  𝜃∈[0,12𝜋)

    Also[5, p. 16],

    dΩ=sin⁡𝜃 d𝜃 d𝜙

    Combining the equations gives

    𝑀=∫02𝜋∫012𝜋𝐿cos⁡𝜃𝑠𝑖𝑛𝜃 d𝜃 d𝜙=𝐿∫02𝜋d𝜙∫012𝜋cos⁡𝜃𝑠𝑖𝑛𝜃 d𝜃=𝐿∫02𝜋d𝜙∫01sin⁡𝜃 d𝑠𝑖𝑛𝜃=𝐿∫02𝜋(12d𝜙)=𝜋𝐿

    Therefore[14],

    𝐿=𝑀𝜋

    Let the rated luminous flux of the projector be 𝐹. If only one projector is used, it must satisfy

    𝐹≥𝑀𝐴

    If 𝑁 identical projectors are used, and each uniformly illuminates the entire screen while their luminous-flux outputs add linearly, the required luminous flux of each projector is at least

    𝐹≥𝑀𝐴𝑁

    In reality, of course, a screen cannot reflect all incident light, and a projector’s actual luminous-flux output may not always reach its rated value. The rated luminous flux actually required from each projector is therefore usually greater than 𝐹. This result is only an ideal lower bound, and appropriate headroom should be allowed when selecting equipment.

    Modern cinemas also commonly use high-gain screens. These provide greater luminance than a Lambertian screen to viewers looking from near the screen’s normal direction, at the cost of reduced luminance at larger viewing angles. In general, a screen’s gain depends not only on the viewing direction but also on the incident direction of the light. These involve more complex mathematical methods and are not discussed here.

    Examples

    Section titled “Examples”

    Projection luminance is commonly expressed in foot-lamberts, fL. The conversion between fL and nit is

    1fL=(1𝜋)cd/ft2≈3.42626cd/m2=3.42626nit,1nit≈0.291864fL

    Thus, 31fL≈106.214nit and 22fL≈75.3777nit. For the ideal Lambertian screen assumed in this section, a target luminance of 1fL corresponds to a luminous exitance of 1lm/ft2. The numerical luminance value in fL can therefore be used directly as the luminous-exitance value in lm/ft2 for these calculations.

    Example 1: A cinema auditorium has a screen measuring 22m×12m. The required projection luminance for 2D films is 31fL, and 2 projectors are used. The screen area and corresponding luminous exitance are

    𝐴=(22m)×(12m)=264m2≈2841.672350ft2,𝑀=31lm/ft2

    Therefore, each projector must provide a luminous flux of

    𝐹≥𝑀𝐴2=(31lm/ft2)(2841.672350ft2)2≈44046lm

    In theory, each projector must provide at least approximately 44000lm of luminous flux.

    Example 2: A cinema auditorium has a screen measuring 28m×21m. The required projection luminance for 2D films is 22fL, and 2 projectors are used. The screen area and corresponding luminous exitance are

    𝐴=(28m)×(21m)=588m2≈6329.179325ft2,𝑀=22lm/ft2

    Therefore, each projector must provide a luminous flux of

    𝐹≥𝑀𝐴2=(22lm/ft2)(6329.179325ft2)2≈69621lm

    In theory, each projector must provide at least approximately 70000lm of luminous flux. This already exceeds the maximum luminous flux of almost all cinema projectors, such as DP4K-60L, so a custom projector or a high-gain screen is almost certainly required.

    Example 3: A cinema auditorium has a screen measuring 19m×10m. The required projection luminance for 2D films is 22fL, and 1 projector is used. The screen area and corresponding luminous exitance are

    𝐴=(19m)×(10m)=190m2≈2045.142979ft2,𝑀=22lm/ft2

    Therefore, the projector must provide a luminous flux of

    𝐹≥𝑀𝐴=(22lm/ft2)(2045.142979ft2)≈44993lm

    In theory, the projector must provide at least approximately 45000lm of luminous flux.
